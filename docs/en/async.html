THEN

Album

async

22 May 2025

“I tried to forget every convention and rule. I just wanted to put in the sounds, the things I wanted to hear. It doesn’t matter. This might be my last one.”

For five full years Ryuichi Sakamoto had no inspiration left for a new album — not since ‘Out of Noise’ in 2009.

So he went back to writing film scores in the meantime, while looking for something to hold on to, until in 2014 he began sketching out ideas for a new album that were finally taking shape. Then everything stopped when he was diagnosed with throat cancer, at the age of 62.

All the work of building the songs came to a halt. The following year, during his treatment, Sakamoto thought that the new album he was making would be his last.

That was when he began to conceive of ‘async’. Only one song had been written before the cancer diagnosis: ‘andata’, the album’s opening track.

Sakamoto wanted to talk about a-synchronism — about the thin line between life, synthesised sound, and music.

“I just wanted to hear sounds — the sounds of everyday things, from ordinary tools all the way to musical instruments.”

Sakamoto made and recorded a great many sounds, natural and electronic alike. He even used a piano whose tuning had been ruined after being submerged in the 2011 tsunami; its sound appears in the track “ZURE” on the album.

“I think it was ‘nature’ that put the piano out of tune. The piano is an instrument full of system and structure, designed in fine detail by human hands and arranged with the utmost care so that the scale comes out correct. But those pianos are part of nature too. The thing is, if you leave one alone long enough without tuning it, it will go out of tune in the end.”

Sakamoto also tied the album’s central idea to a great many notions about human society.

“By human nature, most people feel happy when they feel in sync with society. That’s why I wanted to make music that doesn’t follow convention — like a language that doesn’t exist. But the hard part is that I still wanted it to be music you can listen to.”

In async, none of the sounds that appear are ever in agreement, never producing anything sweet to the ear. But Ryuichi gives his reason: “As human beings, we have the freedom to say which sounds are good and which are bad. I would suggest instead that we try listening again.

In a human world of differing thoughts, we look for a faction that shares our interests, we join with the group that holds one idea in common, we push and explore that idea to its limit — but we take no interest in any thought outside our own, and what remains is nothing but mutual mistrust.” It is just like the sounds in async: various and different, never converging, never combining into anything sweet.

‘async’ (2017) is considered one of Ryuichi Sakamoto’s finest albums, and one reason may well be the ‘fear of death’ taking shape inside him — forcing him to do everything he could, to say what was in his heart in as many forms as possible, to give everything he had, because he could not know which track would be his last.

Ryuichi Sakamoto once collaborated with the Thai director Apichatpong Weerasethakul, maker of ‘Uncle Boonmee Who Can Recall His Past Lives’ (2010), on the work ‘async–first light’ (2017).

“Because we never know when we will die, we have the feeling that life is a well that never runs dry. How many more times do you think you will see the full moon rise? Perhaps twenty. And yet you still feel you have all the time in the world.”
