THEN

Album

async

22 May 2025

“I tried to forget every convention and every rule. I just wanted to put in the sounds, the things I wanted to hear. It doesn’t matter. This might be my last one.”

For five straight years Ryuichi Sakamoto had nothing left in him for a new album — not since ‘Out of Noise’ in 2009.

So he went back to scoring films while he waited for something to arrive, until in 2014 the sketches for a new album finally started taking shape. Then everything stopped: he was diagnosed with throat cancer, at sixty-two.

All the building came to a halt. The following year, in the middle of treatment, Sakamoto decided the album he was making would be his last.

That was where ‘async’ began. Only one song had been written before the diagnosis: ‘andata’, the album’s opening track.

What Sakamoto wanted to talk about was a-synchronism — the thin line between life, synthesised sound, and music.

“I just wanted to hear sounds. The sounds of ordinary things, from everyday tools all the way up to instruments.”

He made and recorded an enormous number of them, natural and electronic alike. He even used a piano whose tuning had been wrecked after going under in the 2011 tsunami; you can hear it on the track “ZURE.”

“I think it was nature that put that piano out of tune. A piano is an instrument full of system and structure, designed in fine detail by human hands, arranged with the greatest possible care so the scale comes out right. But pianos are part of nature too. Leave one alone long enough without tuning it, and it will go out of tune in the end.”

Sakamoto also tied the album’s central idea to a great deal of thinking about human society.

“By nature most people feel happy when they feel in sync with society. That’s why I wanted to make music that doesn’t follow the conventions — something like a language that doesn’t exist. The hard part was that I still wanted it to be music you can actually listen to.”

Nothing in async ever quite agrees with anything else; it never produces anything sweet. But Ryuichi has his reasons: “As human beings we have the freedom to decide which sounds are good and which are bad. What I’d suggest instead is that we try listening again.

In a human world of differing ideas, we look for the faction that shares our interests, join the group that holds one thought in common, push that thought to its limit and take no interest in anything outside it — and what is left is nothing but mutual mistrust.” Which is exactly what the sounds on async do: various, different, never converging, never resolving into something pleasant.

‘async’ (2017) is regarded as one of Sakamoto’s finest records, and part of the reason is surely the fear of death taking shape in him — forcing him to try everything, to say what he had to say in as many forms as he could, to hold nothing back, because there was no way of knowing which track would be the last.

Sakamoto once worked with the Thai director Apichatpong Weerasethakul, who made ‘Uncle Boonmee Who Can Recall His Past Lives’ (2010), on the piece ‘async–first light’ (2017).

“Because we never know when we’ll die, we have the feeling that life is a well that never runs dry. How many more full moons do you think you’ll see rise? Perhaps twenty. And still you feel you have all the time in the world.”
