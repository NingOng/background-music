How Jeff Buckley inspired Fake Plastic Trees

18 December 2024

“…She looks like the real thing
She tastes like the real thing
My fake plastic love...”

Radiohead, one of the great alternative rock bands of the nineties, is familiar to most people through their permanent hit ‘Creep’, from ‘Pablo Honey’ (1993).

After ‘Creep’ went as far as it did, the band found themselves under enormous pressure recording their second album, ‘The Bends’.

Of the sessions for ‘Fake Plastic Trees’, guitarist Ed O’Brien said: “The early takes of Fake Plastic Trees sounded like November Rain by Guns N’ Roses. It was awful.”

One evening they decided to go and see Jeff Buckley at a live club in London, playing ‘Grace’ (1994). The show left a mark on all of them, and on Thom Yorke in particular: “In the early days I thought singing meant being loud, putting a lot of power behind it. After watching Jeff Buckley, he gave me the nerve to sing falsetto.”

Worth adding: there are two ways up into the head register. One is head voice, which is powerful, and where the singer feels the vibration around the head. The other is falsetto, thinner and softer, where the singer feels it around the throat.

Bassist Colin Greenwood put it this way: “After we got back from Jeff’s show we recorded Fake Plastic Trees again. Thom sat down and played it acoustic, and on the third take, when he finished, he burst into tears. That’s the take we used.”

In 1995 Radiohead released ‘Fake Plastic Trees’ on ‘The Bends’, and put an end to the idea that they were a band with one song in them.

Jeff Buckley drowned in 1997, which left ‘Grace’ as the only album released in his lifetime — and so it became the record that stands for him. A voice soft and powerful at once, one that puts the listener under and pulls them down into whatever grief the song is carrying. Jeff was a singer with a sound nobody else had, and he is still working on Thom Yorke and on countless other singers around the world.
