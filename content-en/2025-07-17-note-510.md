คำบางคำ ; Sqweez Animal ; 2011

It is almost ten years since Singh Musikapong, the guitarist of Sqweez Animal, died — and it happens that I have only just started listening to them properly.

So this post is meant as a dedication to Sqweez Animal, through one song. I spent a long time deciding which one, and in the end came back around to the one I have always loved most: “คำบางคำ” (Some Words).

คำบางคำ is from their second album — or, put another way, their last: “ไม่มีที่มา” (2011).

Win, the singer, has described the way he and Singh worked as a kind of conversation. Win would put down the frame, the words, the melody; Singh would take all of it away, sit with it alone and let it drift, and hand it back. Back and forth until the song was done.

“Most of the time the two of us didn’t burn out at the same moment. If one of us was flagging, the other would push a bit and hold him up. Doing it on your own could get rough,” Win said.

It was in the interviews for that second album that Singh described what they made as psychedelic soul: rhythm with groove in it, and at the same time something sweet and hazy for the listener to float away on.

One thing that makes คำบางคำ unusual is that it is among the songs the French indie-pop band Tahiti 80 helped produce.

But the thing that really sets it apart is how much of himself Singh put into it. He loved this song. He wanted to write it himself, and even wanted to sing it himself, because the words and the shape of the whole thing had got hold of him.

“I miss you again, and more every time.
I have only just understood how much I love you — only just understood that it is everything I have.”

“There must be some words I have said to you that made you angry with me.
There must be days when the love between us is forgotten. Please forgive me.”

“Singh had one of the most distinctive guitar voices of anyone I know. His ears heard things that I, and everyone I’ve known, simply don’t hear that way,” said Karn of The Parkinson in an interview.

I have no doubt Sqweez Animal’s songs are still working on the Thai bands that came after them — whether through Win’s high but weighted voice or Singh’s sweet, half-dreaming guitar.

Ten years on, however long Singh has been gone, however far away he has got to, his guitar keeps ringing after the song ends, every time we put it on.
