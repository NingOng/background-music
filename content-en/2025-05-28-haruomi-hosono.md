“Good timing always happens to me, it drives things forward. I’m lazy, I don’t do anything, just make a wish.”

Haruomi Hosono is a Japanese musician who has had an enormous influence on Japanese music and on music worldwide.

He travelled from being the bassist of a folk band to a pioneer of techno, and one of the founders of the Yellow Magic Orchestra, or YMO.

𝗦𝘁𝗮𝗿𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗕𝗮𝗻𝗱 𝗖𝗮𝗿𝗲𝗲𝗿

“I was born just two years after the Second World War ended. That meant that growing up, I absorbed a great deal of Western music and American influence.”

Hosono began his career in music as the bassist of the psychedelic rock band Apryl Fool, before leaving to join the folk-rock band Happy End.

Hosono also sang a song called “Kaze wo Atsumete” (1971), which was later used in the romance film “Lost in Translation” (2003).

“My voice is quite low, but my bandmates forced me to sing. So I studied Western singers who sang low — how they did it. Tom Rush, that sort of thing.”

‘𝗘𝘅𝗼𝘁𝗶𝗰𝗮’ 𝗠𝗼𝘃𝗲𝗺𝗲𝗻𝘁

After parting ways with Happy End, Hosono began making his own solo albums, mostly in the exotica style — that is, the image of being on a far-off tropical island in a vast sea, with music that gives you that feeling.

One of his most memorable exotica albums is “Pacific” (1978), co-produced with his old friend from Happy End, Shigeru Suzuki, and with the king of city pop, Tatsuro Yamashita.

The important turning point was working with two friends who would later become members of YMO: Yukihiro Takahashi and Ryuichi Sakamoto.

They collaborated on the album “Paraiso” (1978), a significant turning point because it began to mix exotica, melodies resembling Japanese folk music, and various electronic sounds.

‘𝗧𝗲𝗰𝗵𝗻𝗼’ 𝗠𝗼𝘃𝗲𝗺𝗲𝗻𝘁

In 1978, Hosono, Takahashi, and Sakamoto together founded the Yellow Magic Orchestra, or YMO. In that same era the German band Kraftwerk was likewise beginning to make techno.

But where Kraftwerk interpreted techno in a dystopian mode, YMO wanted their techno to come out playful and free.

While working with YMO, Hosono released a solo album called “Philharmony” (1982), an experiment with the E-mu Emulator — a machine that could sample recorded sounds, alter them, and play them from a keyboard.

‘Philharmony’ has a hit track, “スポーツマン” (Sports Men), which personally startled me a great deal on first listen, because the sounds are so modern it is hard to believe they were recorded back in 1982.

YMO designed a great many sounds that have been handed down to the present day — in electronic music, in hip-hop, and in the sounds of arcade games.

‘𝗔𝗺𝗯𝗶𝗲𝗻𝘁’ 𝗠𝗼𝘃𝗲𝗺𝗲𝗻𝘁

In 1984 the members of YMO decided to go their separate ways and make their own solo albums. This was the beginning of Hosono and the sounds of nature.

“Generally I’m a shy person. I had a terrible time after YMO blew up. It was like being a baseball player — I couldn’t go anywhere. I just wanted to be free.” That was probably the reason he began experimenting with ambient music.

For the album ‘Paradise View’ (1985), Hosono had the chance to travel to Okinawa, where he explored the sounds of folk instruments and the chanting at temples in the local communities.

Out of that journey he distilled ‘Paradise View’ — the image of a place he wished existed, and wanted to see with his own eyes at least once.

That same year, Hosono travelled to Nara Prefecture, one fifth of which is designated national park.

He went to a small village called Tenkawa, where he watched the stars drift across a silent sky, listened to birds, and heard the lulling sound of folk instruments.

Out of it came the album ‘Mercuric Dance’ (1985), whose tracks are broken down into the names of the planets, each slowly orbiting the sun — some dancing, some transforming, sounding out as they drift across a planetarium sky, seen from where we stand.

The album with the clearest natural sounds is probably ‘MEDICINE COMPILATION from the quiet lodge’, which is like lifting a hut from the middle of a great forest and placing it inside an album — as though nature had gathered up its medicines and sealed them at the heart of this hut.

The song ‘HONEY MOON’ on that album was in fact written by Hosono back when he was still making island music, or exotica, for the album ‘Tropical Dandy’ (1975). But Hosono brought the song forward and reinterpreted it — quieter now, with ambient textures and harmony vocals added.

𝗔𝗳𝘁𝗲𝗿 𝗔𝗺𝗯𝗶𝗲𝗻𝘁

On his later albums (the 2010s), Hosono returned to easy-listening pop, or else recorded covers of old songs.

You can see that once he decided which kind of music interested him, he never clung to the old things, and always chose to do whatever interested him most at that moment.

“Good timing always happens to me. Timing drives everything forward. I’m a lazy person, I don’t much like doing anything — I just sit and wish for what I’m thinking of to come true.”

“My future is narrowing, and I can’t go back to the past. Twentieth-century music was my whole life… ‘It will be forgotten.’ Extinct, you could say. So now I want to do the things I once came across, the things that made me feel joy again.”

Today Hosono is the only remaining member of YMO, following the deaths of Takahashi and Sakamoto in 2023.

“It is a great honour that YMO has been proposed as a symbol [for MUSIC AWARDS JAPAN 2025] for the first time.”

“There are two other members who could not be here due to personal circumstances: Takahashi Yukihiro and Sakamoto Ryuichi. Without their gifts, I would not be standing here,” Hosono said while opening Japan’s annual music awards.

"Not everyone is happy in the profession they take up. They have to train just as hard as athletes.

If I suddenly wanted to be a singer, I would have to train my throat hard. And the worst thing is — what if my throat became so sore I couldn’t sing any more?

So I want to do things comfortably, without rushing. The most important thing for me as a musician is to be happy with the music."
