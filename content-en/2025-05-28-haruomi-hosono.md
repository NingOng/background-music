“Good timing always happens to me, it drives things forward. I’m lazy, I don’t do anything, just make a wish.”

Haruomi Hosono is a Japanese musician whose influence on music in Japan and around the world is hard to overstate.

He went from bassist in a folk band to a pioneer of techno and one of the founders of the Yellow Magic Orchestra — YMO.

𝗦𝘁𝗮𝗿𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗕𝗮𝗻𝗱 𝗖𝗮𝗿𝗲𝗲𝗿

“I was born two years after the Second World War ended. Which meant that growing up, I soaked up a lot of Western music and American influence.”

Hosono began as bassist in the psychedelic rock band Apryl Fool, then left to join the folk-rock band Happy End.

He also sang a song called “Kaze wo Atsumete” (1971), later used in the film “Lost in Translation” (2003).

“My voice is pretty low, but the others in the band made me sing. So I studied how Western singers with low voices did it. Tom Rush, that sort of thing.”

‘𝗘𝘅𝗼𝘁𝗶𝗰𝗮’ 𝗠𝗼𝘃𝗲𝗺𝗲𝗻𝘁

After Happy End split, Hosono started making solo albums, mostly exotica — that is, music built around the image of being on some far-off tropical island in the middle of an enormous sea, and sounding like it.

One of his best-remembered exotica records is “Pacific” (1978), co-produced with his old Happy End bandmate Shigeru Suzuki and with the king of city pop, Tatsuro Yamashita.

The turn came with two friends who would later be in YMO: Yukihiro Takahashi and Ryuichi Sakamoto.

They worked together on “Paraiso” (1978), a genuine turning point, because it starts mixing exotica, melodies that lean toward Japanese folk music, and electronics.

‘𝗧𝗲𝗰𝗵𝗻𝗼’ 𝗠𝗼𝘃𝗲𝗺𝗲𝗻𝘁

In 1978, Hosono, Takahashi, and Sakamoto founded the Yellow Magic Orchestra. Over in Germany, Kraftwerk were making techno at the same moment.

But where Kraftwerk read techno as dystopian, YMO wanted theirs to come out playful and free.

While YMO was running, Hosono put out a solo album called “Philharmony” (1982), an experiment with the E-mu Emulator — a machine that could sample recorded sound, reshape it, and play it back from a keyboard.

‘Philharmony’ has a hit in “スポーツマン” (Sports Men), which frankly startled me the first time I heard it: the sounds are so modern it is hard to believe the record dates from 1982.

YMO designed a great deal of sound that has come down to the present, in electronic music, in hip-hop, and in the noise of arcade games.

‘𝗔𝗺𝗯𝗶𝗲𝗻𝘁’ 𝗠𝗼𝘃𝗲𝗺𝗲𝗻𝘁

In 1984 the members of YMO decided to go their own ways and make solo records. This is where Hosono and the sounds of the natural world begin.

“I’m a shy person, generally. I had an awful time after YMO blew up. It was like being a baseball player — I couldn’t go anywhere. I just wanted to be free.” Which is probably why he started experimenting with ambient music.

For ‘Paradise View’ (1985), Hosono travelled to Okinawa and went looking for the sound of folk instruments and the chanting at temples in the local communities.

Out of that trip he distilled ‘Paradise View’: a picture of a place he wished existed, and wanted to see with his own eyes at least once.

That same year he travelled to Nara Prefecture, a fifth of which is national park.

He went to a small village called Tenkawa, where he watched the stars move across a silent sky, listened to birds, and heard folk instruments playing something close to a lullaby.

What came out was ‘Mercuric Dance’ (1985), its tracks broken out into the names of the planets, each one slowly circling the sun — some of them dancing, some of them changing, sounding out as they drift across a planetarium sky, seen from where we happen to stand.

The album with the clearest natural sound is probably ‘MEDICINE COMPILATION from the quiet lodge’, which is rather like lifting a hut out of the middle of a great forest and setting it down inside a record — as though nature had gathered up its medicines and sealed them in the middle of it.

The song ‘HONEY MOON’ on that album goes back to his island-music days, on ‘Tropical Dandy’ (1975). Hosono brought it forward and read it again, quieter now, with ambient textures and harmony vocals laid over the top.

𝗔𝗳𝘁𝗲𝗿 𝗔𝗺𝗯𝗶𝗲𝗻𝘁

On his later records, through the 2010s, Hosono went back to easy pop, or recorded covers of old songs.

You can see that once he settled on whatever music had his attention, he never held on to the old thing, and always did whatever interested him most right then.

“Good timing always happens to me. Timing drives everything forward. I’m a lazy person, I don’t much like doing anything — I just sit and wish for what I’m thinking about to come true.”

“My future is getting narrower, and I can’t go back. Twentieth-century music was my whole life… it will be forgotten. Extinct, you could say. So now I want to do the things I once came across, the things that made it fun again.”

Hosono is the only member of YMO still living, after the deaths of Takahashi and Sakamoto in 2023.

“It’s a great honour that YMO has been proposed as a symbol [for MUSIC AWARDS JAPAN 2025] for the first time.”

“There are two other members who couldn’t be here for reasons of their own: Takahashi Yukihiro and Sakamoto Ryuichi. Without what they had, I wouldn’t be standing here,” Hosono said, opening Japan’s annual music awards.

"Not everyone is happy in the work they take up. They have to train as hard as athletes do.

If I suddenly decided to be a singer, I’d have to work my throat hard. And the worst of it is — what if my throat got so sore I couldn’t sing any more?

So I want to do things comfortably, without rushing. The most important thing for me as a musician is being happy with the music."
