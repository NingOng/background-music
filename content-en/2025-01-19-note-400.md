“If I cannot bring you down, I will climb up into the sky. Lend me a ladder…”

I suspect a fair number of teenagers with a taste for alternative music have heard that line. It closes “Acrophobia,” the best-known song by Penguin Villa — that is, Jay Jetamon, once the guitarist of Praw, the iconic alternative band of the nineties.

‘Acrophobia’ is about reaching for someone you are drawn to. However impossible it looks, you might as well ask for a ladder and see how far up you get. Between the melody and the guitar effects, it is fair to call it Penguin Villa’s signature song.

The very next track on the same album, though, is one of the bleakest things they have ever recorded: “กลับไปที่โลก” (Back to Earth).

“…A past where we understood each other, and a present that has changed. Now, when you come close, what I feel is exhaustion…”

“กลับไปที่โลก” appears on the album ‘ออกไปข้างนอก’ (2017). After all the head-nodding of Acrophobia, plenty of listeners — more than a few, I would guess — have lost a tear or two to the track that follows.

It came about, Jay has said, during a stretch when he was playing a lot of shows and needed more material. He had written something cool with a memorable closing line in Acrophobia; now he wanted a sad song that was cool as well.

One of the tricks he uses here is a key change. Most songs change key in the bridge — the passage leading into the hook — or else right at the end, and the change is meant to be heard. You are supposed to notice: right, we have moved.

Jay went the other way and changed key at the top of the hook. The method is to sing the last note of the bridge over a chord that contains it in one key, then, as the hook lands, switch to a chord in a different key while the sung note stays exactly where it was. The move becomes invisible. It is like walking along chatting with you — I say, right, let us take a stroll in the garden, I keep you talking, and by the time you look up I have walked you deep into the forest and you never noticed a thing.

The only other place I can think of that does this is ‘After the Love Has Gone’ by Earth, Wind & Fire. The usual result of changing key at the top of the hook is that singers trying it for the first time cannot find the key and drift off the original, because it never occurred to them that the ground had shifted.

Beyond the technique, what makes the song land is a lyric most people will find far too easy to go along with — until their own life gets mixed in with it and it becomes hard to say where the song stops and the truth starts.

If you have never tried it, go and listen to ‘Acrophobia’ and see whether it talks you into messaging the person you like.

Then try ‘กลับไปที่โลก’ and ‘After the Love Has Gone’: turn the song off, sing it back, and see whether you are still in the same key as the record.

Or just listen to ‘กลับไปที่โลก’ and see how much of yourself you find in it.

“Even if we love each other, the heaven we built together may be something we have to leave behind — and go back, each of us, to a world of our own.”
