“หากไม่อาจลงมา จะปีนขึ้นไปบนฟ้า ขอบันไดหน่อย…”

I expect no small number of alternative-leaning teenagers have heard this line, the closing passage of “Acrophobia,” the famous song by Penguin Villa — that is, Jay Jetamon, former guitarist of Praw, the iconic alternative band of the nineties.

‘Acrophobia’ tells of the effort to reach out for the person we are drawn to. However impossible it may seem, we will just have to ask for a ladder and try climbing up. Together with the melody and the various guitar effects, this song could fairly be called Penguin Villa’s signature.

The next song on that same album, however, is one of Penguin Villa’s most quietly devastating: “กลับไปที่โลก” (Back to Earth).

“…อดีตที่มีแต่ความเข้าใจ ทว่าปัจจุบันเปลี่ยน ในเวลาที่เธอเข้ามาใกล้ กลับกลายเป็นความอ่อนล้า...”

“กลับไปที่โลก” is a song on the album ‘ออกไปข้างนอก’ (2017). After all the head-nodding fun of Acrophobia, many listeners — more than a few, I suspect — will have shed a tear over the track that follows.

It came about because Jay Jetamon has said that during a stretch when he had to play a great many live shows, he needed to write more songs. Having written something cool with a killer line in Acrophobia, he wanted a sad song that was cool as well.

One of the techniques Jay used in this song is the key change. Normally most songs change key during the bridge — that is, the passage before the hook — or else change at the very end of the song. These key changes are meant for the listener to hear and register: right, we've changed key now.

But Jay chose another technique: changing right at the start of the hook. The principle is to sing the last note of the bridge using a chord that contains that note in one key, and then, at the hook, switch to a chord in a different key while the sung word stays on the same note. It makes the key change seamless — like walking along chatting with you, and I say, all right, we're going for a stroll in the garden, and I keep you talking, and by the time you notice I've walked you deep into the forest, and you never even realised.

The only other place I can think of with this technique is ‘After the Love Has Gone’ by Earth, Wind & Fire. The effect of changing key at the start of the hook is usually that singers trying it for the first time can’t find the key and don’t match the original, because they don’t realise the key has already changed.

Beyond the technique described above, one thing that makes this song stand out is lyrics that most people will find it all too easy to go along with — until they slip their own life in among them, and it becomes hard to separate what is the song and what is real.

I would encourage everyone who has not tried it to go and listen to ‘Acrophobia’, and see whether you might open up and message the person you like.

‘กลับไปที่โลก’ and ‘After the Love Has Gone’ — see whether, after listening, you turn the song off and sing along, and whether you match the original.

Or when you listen to ‘กลับไปที่โลก’, see how much you relate to it.
